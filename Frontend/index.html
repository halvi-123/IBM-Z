Know up to 24 hours ahead when a river will cross its high level.

Every hour, the model reads the last 10 days of river level and rainfall and gives the chance of crossing a high river level within 24 hours. It turns that into one clear status. When the sensor data looks broken, it says so instead of reporting a confident "low".

Why river levels

A river is one of the few hazards that warns you before it arrives. Rain falls on the hills first and reaches the gauge hours later. That delay is time responders can use, if someone reads the signs early and the readings can be trusted.

4 Dec, 17:00
The river looks calm

The level sits near 0.80 m, well under the 1.375 m high level. A level alarm sees nothing. Our model raises its first ALERT. By 19:00 the last 6 h of rain is higher than 88% of training hours.

27 h to act
5 Dec, 03:00 to 19:00
The river starts to climb

The level rises from 0.88 m to 1.29 m over 16 hours, with the ALERT on throughout.

5 Dec, 20:00
Crosses the high level

1.42 m and still rising. This is the moment a simple level alarm would first fire.

Real replay of the River Exe at Thorverton from the holdout year, which the model never saw in training. One event: across the 9 events that year the median lead was 11 h.

What responders can do with a day's notice

  • Close fords and low roads before cars get caught, not after.
  • Move vehicles, equipment and livestock out of low-lying ground.
  • Put out temporary barriers and pumps and stand up crews.
  • Check on vulnerable residents while roads are still open.

What they have today, and the gap

  • Live gauge readings show where the river is now, not where it is heading in the next day.
  • Official flood warnings are the authority and stay that way. This is a second pair of eyes on one gauge, with its track record shown next to it.
  • We predict a measurable line, the gauge's high level, rather than "flood". Whether water reaches homes depends on local defences, so we only claim what we can check.

Three things make this hard

Too early or too late

A level alarm fires once the river is already high. Alerting on rain alone fires too often: our own 24 h rain rule caught 33 of 45 events at a higher false alert rate than the model.

False alarms wear out trust

Every alert costs crews time. Too many and people stop responding, so we count false alerts per month next to every result, not just how many events were caught.

Sensors fail quietly

A frozen gauge or a dropped feed looks like calm water. A system that then says "low risk" is worse than no system, so broken data is shown as DATA STALE or DATA ISSUE.

How it works

One scoring path for live data, replay and benchmarks, so what we measure is exactly what we serve.

01

Readings

River level and rainfall from the Environment Agency Hydrology API, cleaned to an hourly grid.

02

Features

Level now, rises over 1 to 24 h, recent peaks, rain over 1 h to 7 days.

03

Model on LinuxONE

HistGradientBoosting gives the chance of crossing in 24 h. Trained and served on s390x.

04

Data checks

Stale feed, missing hours, stuck sensor, sudden spike, rain gauge offline.

05

Status

One status with reasons and data warnings. Balanced or sensitive mode.

What it does well

  • On the Dec 2024 holdout event, the first ALERT came 27 h before the river crossed the high level.
  • Never turns missing or late data into a confident LOW: it shows DATA STALE or DATA ISSUE.
  • In testing, hours given about 30% crossed about 30% of the time.
  • About 8 ms from data to status on LinuxONE, reported as measured.

Known limits

  • One gauge and 45 events: results are noisy and models are within noise of each other.
  • A stuck sensor takes 24 h to detect. It is the one silent miss in our SYNTHETIC stress tests.
  • Reasons describe unusual inputs. They are not an explanation of the model.
  • Decision support only. It does not replace official warnings.
